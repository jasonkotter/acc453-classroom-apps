# Instructor guide: Can the Central Bank Be Believed?

**Class:** Tuesday, April 6, after 14.1–14.3 (independence, how the Fed is built, presidents and
the Fed). **Book:** Ch. 12 and 14. **Time:** about 15–20 minutes; replaces part of the
independence discussion and leads into the Turkey 2021–23 case.

## Goal

A central bank that can surprise the public with inflation can lower unemployment, briefly. A
public that sees the surprise coming builds it into its forecasts, and the surprise stops working
while the inflation stays. Students should leave able to explain why a promise matters only if
people act on it, why discretion can produce higher inflation with no lasting gain in jobs, and
what makes a commitment credible.

## How a round works

1. **A policy team of 3** is picked at random each round; everyone else is the **public**,
   setting wages and prices.
2. **Four years, two steps each.** First, each member of the public forecasts this year's
   inflation. Then the team chooses this year's inflation (the median of its members' votes) and
   may announce a plan for next year.
3. **Unemployment = 5% − (inflation − the public's average forecast).** Only a surprise moves it.
4. **Scores.** The public scores the total amount its forecasts missed by. The team scores
   (inflation − 2)² + 2 × (unemployment − 3)² each year. The team wants 2% inflation and
   unemployment below its natural rate of 5%, which is the temptation. Its phone shows a
   calculator of the unemployment and score each inflation choice would give.

No forecasts means last year's inflation; no team answer means 2%.

**The arithmetic.** With a forecast of 2%, the team's best choice is about 3.3%. If the public
sees this coming, inflation settles at 6% with unemployment back at 5%: no jobs gained, only
inflation.

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Join and rules | Read the opening script. |
| 5 min | 1. Discretion | Nothing more. |
| 4 min | 2. Commitment | "A new team. This time each year's announced plan becomes next year's inflation. The team can't change it after you forecast." |
| 4 min | 3. A conservative central banker | Optional. "A new team that chooses each year again, but is scored on inflation only." |
| 4 min | Debrief | |

Each round: **Open year 1 forecasts**, then alternate the forecast and policy steps through the
last year, then **Reveal**. Give forecasts about 20 seconds and the team about 20 seconds.

## Setup

Create the session, open the projector screen on the classroom display, and wait for the joined
count to match the room. Tell the room that three people will be picked as the central bank, so
nobody is surprised.

## Opening script

"Three of you are about to run the central bank. Everyone else sets wages and prices, so you need
to forecast inflation. If inflation comes in above what you expected, unemployment falls for a
year. The central bank wants 2% inflation, but it also wants unemployment below normal. Each year
it will tell you its plan. You decide whether to believe it."

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. With 40 simulated students who mostly extrapolate last year's
inflation, discretion produced inflation of 3.5% rising to 5% (average 4.4%) while the team
always announced 2%, and unemployment drifted back toward 5%. Commitment gave 2.0% inflation and
5.0% unemployment every year. The conservative banker gave 2.0% inflation and 5.4% unemployment.
Real students may trust the team longer, or stop trusting it faster, than the simulated ones.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- When did the class stop believing the announcements, and why?
- Did surprise inflation buy lasting jobs?
- What made the commitment round credible? What real institutions play that role
  (independence, mandates, inflation targets, a central banker's reputation)?
- Who did better under the conservative banker, and who did worse?
- Turkey 2021–23: what happens to expectations when the central bank is seen as taking orders?

The game illustrates a mechanism; it does not show that independence alone explains any
country's inflation.

## Fallback without the app

Forecasts on slips; three volunteers choose inflation at the front, with the team's calculator
table on the board.
