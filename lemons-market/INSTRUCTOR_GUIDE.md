# Instructor guide: Lemons Market

**Class:** Tuesday, February 9, after 06.2–06.4 (hidden type and hidden action, why banks exist,
screening and monitoring). **Book:** Ch. 6. **Time:** about 20 minutes, then Hidden Effort if
there is time.

## Goal

Every car in this market is worth more to a buyer than to its seller, so every sale should
happen. Only the seller knows the car's quality, though, and buyers who have been burned offer
less, which pushes the good cars out. Students should leave able to explain adverse selection
from what they watched happen, and why credible information (inspection, warranties, credit
scores) brings trade back.

## How a round works

1. **Sellers and buyers.** The class is split at random into sellers and buyers, paired one to
   one. With an odd number, one student watches that round.
2. **Each car is one of five qualities**, equally likely: Lemon ($2,000 to its seller), Rough
   ($4,000), Fair ($6,000), Good ($8,000), and Peach ($10,000). A buyer values any car at 1.5 times
   its value to the seller. Only the seller knows the quality.
3. **The buyer makes one offer**; the seller accepts or rejects. No offer or no answer means no
   sale.
4. **Inspection round.** Before offers, a seller may pay $500 to have the car inspected, and the
   buyer then sees the quality.

New cars and new partners every round. With 55 students there are 27 pairs and one watcher.

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Join and rules | Read the opening script. |
| 4 min | 1. Hidden quality | Nothing more. |
| 3 min | 2. Hidden quality, round 2 | Point at "average quality sold" from round 1. "Buyers, you now know what sold last time." |
| 3 min | 3. Hidden quality, round 3 | "Watch whether the good cars keep leaving." |
| 4 min | 4. Inspection | "Sellers can now pay $500 to have the car inspected. The buyer sees the result." |
| 4 min | Debrief | |

Each round: **Open the market**, **End offers; send them to sellers**, **Close the market**,
**Reveal**. The inspection round adds **End inspections; on to offers** first. Give each step
about 30 seconds.

## Setup

Create the session, open the projector screen on the classroom display, and wait for the joined
count to match the room. Students who join late play from the next round.

## Opening script

"Half of you are selling a used car, and half of you are buying one. Every car is worth more to
the buyer than to the seller, so every one of these sales would make both people better off. The
seller knows how good the car is. The buyer doesn't. Buyers, make one offer. Sellers, accept or
reject it."

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. With 60 simulated students, the average quality sold fell
from $4,000 to $3,250 to $2,286 over the three hidden rounds, and the share of possible gains
realized fell from 43% to 9%; with inspection, average quality sold rose to $6,000 and 80% of the
gains were realized. In the live rehearsal with 12 bots, 5 of 6 cars sold in round 1, 1 of 6 in
rounds 2 and 3, and 5 of 6 with inspection. Real students will be noisier; the direction is what
matters. If round 1 already looks like a lemons market, play round 2 anyway, because the
decline across rounds is the clearest evidence.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- Which cars stopped selling, and why? Who decided that they would not sell?
- Buyers: how did you choose your offer in round 2? What did round 1 teach you?
- What did inspection change, and who paid for it? Why were the owners of Peaches happy to pay?
- Where do inspection, warranties, or reputation do this job in real markets (CarFax, certified
  pre-owned, credit scores)?
- How does a bank face the same problem with borrowers (06.3), and why is it better placed to
  solve it than you are?

## Fallback without the app

Hand sellers cards marked with a quality; buyers write offers on slips; sellers accept or reject;
tally the qualities that sold on the board after each round.
