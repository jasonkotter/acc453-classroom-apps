# Instructor guide: Fire Sale

**Class:** Tuesday, March 2 (with the First Republic case) or Tuesday, March 9 (with the speed of
runs case). **Book:** Ch. 8–9. **Time:** about 15–20 minutes.

## Goal

Bank Run deliberately has one bank. Fire Sale puts the whole class in the same market: every bank
holds the same long-term asset, and when banks sell to meet withdrawals, the price falls and
every bank's holdings are marked down. Students should leave able to separate a shared shock
(withdrawals) from the damage banks do to each other by selling, explain why a lower market value
became a realized loss here, and say why a central bank lends against good collateral at
pre-crisis values.

## How a round works

1. **Every student is a bank** with $10 of cash and 90 units of the asset at $1.00, funded by
   deposits and $6–$14 of equity. Banks differ, so the thinnest fail first.
2. **Three steps.** Each step a bank sees its withdrawal ($3–$9) and chooses how many units to
   sell. No answer means sell nothing.
3. **The price rule:** every 1% of all the asset in the room sold in a step lowers the price
   0.5%, and every bank's units are marked at the new price.
4. **Forced sales.** A bank that still cannot pay has the shortfall sold for it at a 20%
   discount. A bank whose equity falls below $4 loses $8 more to nervous depositors next step.
5. **Failure.** Equity below zero at the end means the bank failed.
6. **A prediction.** In step 1, everyone predicts the asset's price at the end.

The same withdrawals and balance sheets repeat in every round, so the rounds differ only in the
rules and in what students chose.

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Join and rules | Read the opening script. |
| 4 min | 1. Sales move the price | Nothing more. |
| 4 min | 2. Same banks, no price impact | "Same banks, same withdrawals. This time selling doesn't move the price." |
| 4 min | 3. Sales move the price; the central bank lends | "Selling moves the price again. But you can borrow from the central bank against your units at 70% of what they were worth before the crisis, for 2%." |
| 4 min | Debrief | |

Each round: **Open step 1**, **End step 1; sell and reprice**, **End step 2**, **End the last
step**, then **Reveal**. Give each step about 30 seconds.

## Setup

Create the session, open the projector screen on the classroom display, and wait for the joined
count to match the room.

## Opening script

"Each of you runs a bank, and every bank in this room owns the same asset. Every step your
depositors withdraw some money, and you'll see how much before you choose. You can pay from cash,
or sell some of the asset. When the room sells, the price falls, and it falls for everyone's
holdings, not just the seller's. Before we start, predict where the price ends up."

## Reading the results screen

The reveal re-runs every round's **same choices with no price impact** and reports how many banks
would have failed. Read that number next to the actual failures: the gap is what the banks did to
each other.

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. With 40 simulated banks, the price-impact round ended at
84¢ with 30 of 40 failed, none of which would have failed without the price impact; two-thirds of
the units sold were beyond what withdrawals required. With the central bank lending, the price
ended at 94.9¢ and none failed. The live rehearsal with 12 bots ended at 88.7¢ with 4 failures. In
the model, if every bank sells only what its withdrawals need, the price ends near 95¢ and none
fail.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- Which banks failed: the ones that sold, or the ones that held?
- How much of the damage came from withdrawals, and how much from other banks' selling? (Point at
  the no-impact count.)
- A lower market value is not automatically a realized loss or a failure. Why did it become one
  here?
- Why sell early when you expect others to sell?
- What did lending at pre-crisis values change, and why is that Bagehot's rule?
- Connect to First Republic (Mar 2) or the speed of runs (Mar 9). The model has one asset, a
  mechanical price rule, no interbank lending, and no capital rules; say so.

## Fallback without the app

On the board: five banks, a price that falls 2¢ for every unit sold, and a show of hands each
step for how many units each bank sells.
