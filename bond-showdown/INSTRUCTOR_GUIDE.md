# Instructor guide: Bond Showdown

**Class:** Thursday, February 25, after 08.4–08.6 (the bond market model; AFS, HTM, and SVB).
**Book:** Ch. 8. **Time:** about 20 minutes for both rounds.

## Goal

Students know that bond prices fall when rates rise. The game makes them put money on it twice:
first as an investor choosing between a short and a long bond, then as a bank funded by
deposits that can be withdrawn. Students should leave able to explain why the long bond moves so
much more (duration), why reaching for yield is a bet on rates, and how a bank can be solvent at
book value and insolvent at market value, which is the SVB story.

## The bonds

Both pay annual coupons and sell at face value today: a **2-year at 4.0%** and a **10-year at
4.5%**. A rate move shifts both yields by the same amount at once. If rates rise one point, the
2-year loses 1.86% and the 10-year 7.54%; if they fall one point, the 2-year gains 1.91% and the
10-year 8.32%. If rates rise two points, the 2-year loses 3.67% and the 10-year 14.38%.

## Round 1: investor

Students predict how each bond's price moves if rates rise one point, then split $100,000 among
cash, the 2-year, and the 10-year in whole percents that add to 100. At the reveal the rate move
is drawn 50/50 (or set by you), and every portfolio is shown under **both** a rise and a fall,
grouped by 10-year share, so the lesson is exposure rather than luck.

## Round 2: bank

Each bank has $100 of deposits costing 2.5% and $10 of equity, and splits $110 among cash
(earning 3.0%), the 2-year, and the 10-year. If rates hold, the bank earns a year's interest. If
rates rise two points, 30% of deposits are withdrawn, paid first from cash, then by selling
2-year bonds, then 10-year bonds, at market prices. The bank **fails** if it cannot pay the
withdrawals or if its remaining assets at market prices are worth less than the deposits it
still owes. The projector also counts failed banks that would still look solvent with bonds
carried at cost, as held-to-maturity accounting does.

Example: a bank with everything in the 10-year sells $30 of bonds at 85.6 and realizes a $5.04
loss. Its book equity is $4.96, its market equity is −$5.80, and it fails.

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Join | |
| 7 min | 1. Investor | "You have $100,000. First predict what happens to each bond if rates rise a point, then split your money." |
| 7 min | 2. Bank | "Now you're a bank. Your money came from depositors, who can leave." |
| 4 min | Debrief | |

Each round: **Open choices**, **Close choices**, **Reveal**. For round 2, consider setting what
rates do to **Rise** so the failures happen; the table shows both worlds either way.

## Setup

Create the session, open the projector screen on the classroom display, and wait for the joined
count to match the room.

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. In a demo with 40 simulated banks and rising rates, 23 banks
failed in round 2, and all 23 would have looked solvent at cost. Expect a large share of the room
to reach for the 10-year in round 2, because it pays more if rates hold; that is the useful
mistake.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- Why did the 10-year move about four times as much as the 2-year?
- Round 1: which portfolios would you rather have held under each outcome? Did anyone hold a
  portfolio they would be happy with either way?
- Round 2: who reached for yield, and what did it earn if rates held?
- Why did the failed banks look fine at cost? What would an analyst reading the balance sheet
  have needed to see?
- How is this SVB (08.6), and what would deposit insurance or a lender of last resort have
  changed? (Bank Run, Mar 4.)

## Fallback without the app

The bond pricing spreadsheet: put three portfolios on the board (all cash, all 2-year, all
10-year) and reprice them under a one-point rise and fall.
