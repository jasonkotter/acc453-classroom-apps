# Instructor guide: Bond Desk

**Class:** Tuesday, February 23, after 08.1–08.3 (present value, bond prices, and yield to
maturity). **Book:** Ch. 8 §§6–9. **Time:** about 12–14 minutes for round 1 and 5 more for round 2.

## Goal

Students can discount three cash flows. What they usually miss is where the discount rate comes
from and whose rate sets a market price. In Bond Desk everyone can buy the same note, but each
student has a different required return, the rate their money earns elsewhere. Each student first
turns that rate into a present value and then bids. The class's bids form a demand curve on the
projector, and the price lands at the bid of the first student left out. Students should leave
able to say why a bond is worth more to an investor who needs less, why the market yield is the
required return of the marginal investor rather than an average, and why a rise in everyone's
required return lowers the price.

## The note

Three years, $1,000 face value, a 5% annual coupon: it pays $50, $50, and $1,050. Each phone shows
one required return, from 3.0% to 7.0% in half points. New students get the least-used level, in
an order that spreads the range, so even a small room covers it. The PV at each level:

| Required return | 3.0% | 3.5% | 4.0% | 4.5% | 5.0% | 5.5% | 6.0% | 6.5% | 7.0% | 7.5% | 8.0% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PV | $1,056.57 | $1,042.02 | $1,027.75 | $1,013.74 | $1,000.00 | $986.51 | $973.27 | $960.27 | $947.51 | $934.99 | $922.69 |

The 7.0%, 7.5%, and 8.0% students value the note below the $950 minimum, so they should not bid.

## How a round works

1. **Step 1: value the note.** Each student enters the PV at their required return and a
   prediction of the price. Neither is required. When you end the step, each phone shows the
   correct PV and whether the student's answer was within $1. The phone does not show the PV
   before then, so it can't be copied.
2. **Step 2: bid.** A whole-dollar bid from $950 to $1,100, or no bid. Everyone has $1,100 of cash.
3. **The auction.** Notes for sale equal 40% of the students in the round (at least one). The
   highest bids win, and ties at the cutoff go to a seeded priority. **Every winner pays the same
   price: the larger of $950 and the highest losing bid.** If fewer students bid than there are
   notes, every bidder wins at $950.
4. **Score.** A winner scores PV minus the price. Everyone else scores zero; unspent cash keeps
   its value.

Because each student buys at most one note and someone else's bid sets the price, bidding your PV
is a sensible strategy. Shading below it risks missing a note worth more than the price, and
bidding above it risks paying more than the note is worth to you.

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Join and rules | Read the opening script. |
| 3 min | 1. Step 1 (value) | "Work out what the note is worth at the required return on your phone, and predict the price." |
| 2 min | 1. Step 2 (bid) | "Your phone now shows your PV. Bid, or choose no bid. Everyone who wins pays the same price." |
| 4 min | 1. Reveal and explain | |
| 5 min | 2. Every required return one point higher | "Same note, same notes for sale. Every required return in the room is one point higher, yours included. Before you value it, predict the price." |
| 2 min | Debrief | |

Each round: **Open step 1**, **End step 1: show each phone its PV**, **Close bidding**,
**Reveal**. Round 1 uses the *Baseline* preset and round 2 the *Every required return one point
higher* preset. Each student keeps their rank, so the 3.0% student in round 1 is the 4.0% student
in round 2. If time is short, skip round 2 and ask the debrief prediction question instead.

## Setup

Create the session, open the projector screen on the classroom display, and wait for the joined
count to match the room. Students need a calculator or a phone calculator for step 1.

## Opening script

"Everyone here can buy the same three-year note. It pays fifty dollars, fifty dollars, and then a
thousand and fifty. What differs is what your money could earn somewhere else: that's the
required return on your phone. First work out what the note is worth to you. Then decide what to
bid. Everyone who wins pays the same price, set by the highest bid that lost."

## Reading the results screen

The reveal shows the price, the market yield (the yield to maturity at that price), and the first
bidder left out with their required return. Start there: that student's required return and the
market yield should be close, and that is the point of the game.

The chart ranks the bids from highest to lowest and draws each student's PV, also ranked, as a
dashed line, with the price across it. Bids below the dashed line are shading; bids above it are
overbidding. Two counts sit beside the chart: **winners who bid above their PV** (their score is
negative) and **students who missed a note worth more than the price** (they shaded too far). The
tiles also show the class's median prediction, the share of PV answers within $1, and the median
winner's score.

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. If everyone bids their PV, a room of 30–90 clears near
$1,014 (4.5%) in round 1 and $987 (5.5%) in round 2; in a room of 10 it clears at $1,000 (5.00%)
and $973 (6.01%). Real students will probably shade below their PV, which pulls the price down. In
the demo, 40 simulated students who mostly shaded cleared round 1 at $1,000 (5.00%). A run of 20
simulated students plus one by hand cleared at $990 (5.37%) and then $960 (6.51%).

Expect some step 1 answers to be wrong. The usual errors are discounting the $1,050 for one year
instead of three or leaving out the coupons. Ending step 1 fixes this before anyone bids, so a
wrong PV costs nothing in the auction. The share within $1 on the results screen tells you
whether to review the calculation after class.

**If nobody overbids or shades,** ask why bidding your PV makes sense here. **If several winners
overbid,** start with them: their score is negative because they paid more than the note was
worth to them. **If many students missed profitable notes,** ask what they gave up by shading.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- Whose required return set the price? Why is the market yield close to the required return of
  the first student left out, and not the class average?
- The 3.0% student values the note at $1,056.57. Where does their score come from? (They need
  less than the marginal investor.)
- Round 2: every PV fell, so the price fell by roughly $27, or about 2.7%, for a one-point rise.
  Why did the market yield rise by about a point?
- Transfer: a pension fund needs 4%, and a bank's next-best use of the money earns 6%. The note
  sells at $1,000. Who buys? If deposit rates rise, what happens to the bank's required return
  and to what it will pay for the note? (This sets up Bond Showdown on Feb 25.)

**Exit check:** should a 6% investor buy at $1,000? No: the PV at 6% is $973.27, so paying $1,000
earns about 5%, less than the money earns elsewhere. The common mistake is thinking a higher
price means a higher yield.
