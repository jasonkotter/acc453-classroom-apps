Where does the next loan come from?

A loan creates a deposit. If that deposit stays in a bank account, the bank can lend most of it again.

Multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio

This is an idealized limit, not a promise that banks will create exactly this much money in practice.

Round-by-round model

Each round is smaller than the last

The initial reserve injection backs the first loan. After that, banks keep the required reserve share of each new deposit and lend the rest. Loans become deposits elsewhere in the banking system.

One bank or many?

The system view

In reality, a borrower spends the loan and the seller may use another bank. Reserves move between banks to settle payments, but they stay in the banking system in total.

More realistic assumptions

What makes the multiplier smaller?

People may hold some money as cash, and banks may keep excess reserves. Both reduce deposit creation in this simplified model.

Generalized multiplier
Money multiplier10.00×Broad money per dollar of monetary base
(1 + 0%) ÷ (10% + 0% + 0%)

m = (1 + c) ÷ (rr + e + c), where c is currency held by the public per dollar of deposits and e is excess reserves per dollar of deposits. This model assumes stable ratios and focuses on the banking-system accounting; it is not a mechanical forecast of lending.

With no cash leakage or excess reserves, this matches the simple 1 ÷ rr multiplier.

Three things to remember
A bank loan creates a deposit.
Required reserves constrain how many deposits the system can support in the idealized example.
Cash holdings and excess reserves make the generalized multiplier smaller.
