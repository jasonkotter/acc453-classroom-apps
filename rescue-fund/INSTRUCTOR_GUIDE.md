# Instructor guide: The Rescue Fund

**Class:** Tuesday, March 9, after 09.7–09.9 (the Panic of 1907 and the founding of the Fed), in
place of "You're Morgan." **Book:** Ch. 9 §§11–13. **Time:** about 15–18 minutes for both rounds.

## Goal

In 1907 there was no lender of last resort. A trust company that was sound but short of cash
survived only if other banks lent it money, and every bank wanted the rescue to happen with
someone else's cash. Bank Run (Mar 4) puts students in the depositors' seats; here they are the
rescuers. Students should leave able to explain why a rescue that every bank wants can still
fail, why small lenders are tempted to free-ride, what Morgan's announcement of the gap did, and
why a central bank that lends against good collateral to a solvent borrower makes the whole
scramble unnecessary.

## The setup

Commonwealth Trust is **solvent but short of cash**. Every student runs a bank, and the phone shows
which kind:

| | Free cash | Loss if Commonwealth fails | Share of the room |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large bank | 30 | 40 | about 20%, at least two |
| Small bank | 10 | 8 | the rest |

The target **T** is half of all free cash in the room. With 60 students (12 large, 48 small) the
room holds 840 and T is 420. Roles carry over to round 2, and students who join late fill in.

## How a round works

1. **Window 1.** Each bank pledges from 0 to its free cash and predicts total pledges as a
   percentage of T. No answer is a pledge of 0.
2. **Window 2.** Each bank may raise its pledge but not lower it. The phone asks for the new total
   pledge, and the server enforces both limits. No answer leaves the pledge where it was.
3. **All or nothing.** If total pledges P fall short of T, every pledge is refunded, Commonwealth
   fails, and every bank takes its loss. If P reaches T, every bank lends T ÷ P of its pledge.
   The loan is repaid with interest, but tying up cash in a panic costs 15¢ per dollar lent.
4. **Score.** Points lost = 0.15 × the amount lent + the loss if Commonwealth failed. Lower is
   better.

The incentive is easy to state. Pledging costs at most 4.5 points for a large bank and 1.5 for a
small one, against losses of 40 and 8. A pledge only costs anything when the rescue succeeds,
though, and a small bank's 10 rarely decides whether it does. That is the free-rider problem: each
small bank hopes the others will cover the gap.

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Join and rules | Read the opening script. Point out that large and small banks differ. |
| 4 min | 1. Private pledges | "Pledges are hidden. Nobody sees a total until the end." |
| 2 min | 1. Reveal | |
| 4 min | 2. Morgan in the library | "The run has moved to the next trust company. Same banks, same cash, same target. One change: after the first window, everyone sees the total pledged and the gap." |
| 3 min | Compare and explain | |
| 2 min | Transfer and exit check | |

Each round: **Open window 1**, **End window 1** (in round 2 the button reads **End window 1: show
the total**), **Close pledges**, **Reveal**. Round 1 uses the *Private pledges* preset and round 2
*Morgan in the library*. Give each window about a minute.

## Setup

Create the session, open the projector screen on the classroom display, and wait for the joined
count to match the room. Read T off the projector before you start; it depends on who joined.

## Opening script

"It's October 1907, and there's no Federal Reserve. Commonwealth Trust is sound, but its
depositors want their money today, and it doesn't have the cash. Every one of you runs a bank in
the city, and if Commonwealth goes down, the run comes to you next. You can pledge cash to a
rescue pool. If the pool reaches its target, Commonwealth is saved and you get your money back
with interest, though tying it up costs you. If it falls short, everyone gets their pledge back
and Commonwealth fails."

## Your screen

The instructor screen shows the window 1 total in both rounds, marked as shown or not shown to
the class. In round 1 that lets you see, privately, whether the room is on track. Don't announce
it; the comparison between rounds depends on round 1 staying private.

## Reading the results screen

The reveal says whether Commonwealth was saved and draws a meter of total pledges against T, with
a mark for the window 1 total. Below it are pledges by role (how many large and small banks
pledged nothing, some, or all of their cash), average points lost for each role, the room's total,
and what the room would have lost under the other outcome. With 60 students, a failure costs the
room 864 points (every bank's loss), while a success costs 63 (15% of 420). Put those two numbers
side by side: the room loses far more from a failed rescue than from funding one.

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. Large banks probably pledge most of their cash, which covers
well over half of T. Small banks are the swing group. With 60 students, if every large bank
pledges fully, the rest of the gap is 60, or about 12% of small banks' cash. In the calibration,
the rescue then succeeds about a third of the time when 10% of small banks pledge fully and
almost always when 20% do.

In the demo, simulated large banks pledged in full 80% of the time and small banks 20%. With 21
students the private round failed (125 of 145). With 80 students both rounds succeeded, and small
banks pledged 21% of their cash in private against 41% once the gap was shown. Real students will
differ.

**If round 1 succeeds easily,** ask who carried it and whether the small banks needed to pledge
at all; then ask what would happen with a higher target. **If round 1 fails and round 2
succeeds,** that is the cleanest case: ask what the gap told small banks that they didn't know
before. **If both rounds fail,** ask what a small bank would have needed to know to pledge, and go
straight to the transfer question. Round 2 always comes second, after students have seen round
1, so the difference between rounds is suggestive rather than clean.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- Who pledged, as a share of their cash? Why would a small bank that loses 8 pledge nothing?
- Pledging is not a donation: the loan is repaid with interest. What does a pledge actually cost,
  and when?
- What did seeing the gap after window 1 change? Morgan locked the bankers in his library and
  told them the number; why did that help?
- Transfer: a central bank could lend Commonwealth T against its loans, valued at normal prices
  less a haircut, at a penalty rate. What would that do to this game? Suppose instead that Commonwealth's
  loans are worth less than its deposits even at normal prices. Would a pool loan save it, and
  who would bear the loss? (Lending against good collateral to a solvent borrower is Bagehot's
  rule, the same rule as Fire Sale's third round.)

**Exit check:** the pool needs 420, the others have pledged 414, and you are a small bank with 10.
Pledge 6. Closing the gap costs 0.9 and avoids a loss of 8; pledging all 10 also works, but then
you lend about 9.9 instead of 6. The common mistakes are treating your pledge as too small to
matter or treating it as a donation.

**Before class:** check the Knickerbocker and Trust Company of America details you plan to use
against Ch. 9 §13 and the Mar 17 lecture.
