# Instructor guide: Who Bears the Loss?

**Class:** Tuesday, March 9, after 09.7 (deposit insurance and the lender of last resort). **Book:**
Ch. 9 §11 (changing the game) and §14 (the savings and loan crisis). **Time:** about 8–10 minutes
for rounds 1 and 2, in place of one generic insurance and moral-hazard discussion exercise; round 3
adds about 3. Do not run The Rescue Fund the same day; both would be new games on Mar 9.

## Goal

Bank Run (Mar 4) showed what deposit insurance does to depositors: it removes the reason to run.
This activity shows what it does to the bank's owners. With insured deposits and a premium that
does not depend on risk, owners gain from a risky portfolio even when it pays no more on average
than a safe one, because the insurer takes the bad state. Students should leave able to say where
the owners' extra payoff comes from (a transfer from the insurer, not a better investment), why
lenders who can see and price the risk remove that particular subsidy, and why more equity changes
the owners' choice. That last point sets up Thursday's capital lesson.

It is a diagnostic activity, not a game. Nothing a classmate does changes anyone's bank, and only
the predictions are scored.

## The model

Assets cost 100, funded by 90 of deposits and 10 of the owners' equity. The **safe** portfolio pays
105 for sure. The **risky** portfolio pays 125 or 85, each half the time, so it also pays 105 on
average. Owners keep whatever is left after depositors are paid, and they cannot lose more than
their 10. Treat them as caring only about the average payoff.

Each student, as the owner, picks safe or risky, predicts the owners' expected ending equity for
that choice, and says who covers the shortfall if the risky portfolio pays 85. The reveal shows
both outcomes for both portfolios; no coin is flipped.

| Round | Safe: owners expect | Risky: owners expect | Who covers the bad state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Insured deposits, flat premium | 105 − 90 = 15 | ½ × 35 + ½ × 0 = 17.5 | The insurer pays 5, 2.5 on average |
| 2. Lenders see the portfolio and price it | 15 | ½ × 30 + ½ × 0 = 15 | Lenders: promised 95, they get 95 or 85, 90 on average |
| 3. Insured, equity 15 (deposits 85) | 20 | ½ × 40 + ½ × 0 = 20 | No one: 85 covers deposits of 85 |

## Rounds and timing

| Time | Round | What to say before opening |
|---|---|---|
| 1 min | Join and rules | Read the opening script. |
| 2 min | 1. Insured deposits, flat premium | "Your deposits are fully insured, and the premium is the same whatever you pick." |
| 2 min | 1. Reveal | Ask where the extra 2.5 came from before you point at the insurer column. |
| 2 min | 2. Lenders see the portfolio and price it | "No insurance now. Your lenders see your portfolio before they lend, and they need to get 90 back on average. Will more of you pick risky, or fewer?" |
| 2 min | 2. Reveal and exit check | |
| 3 min (optional) | 3. Insured, with more capital | "Back to insured deposits, but you've issued 5 more equity and repaid 5 of deposits." |

Each round: **Open answers**, **Close answers**, **Reveal**. Use the presets in order. Round 3
changes one thing from round 1; the toolkit's comparison card compares it with round 2 and lists
two changes, so say which round to compare it with.

## Opening script

"You own a bank. You've put in 10, depositors have put in 90, and you have 100 to invest. One
portfolio pays 105 for sure. The other pays 125 or 85 on a coin flip, so it also pays 105 on
average. You keep what's left after depositors are paid, and you can't lose more than your 10.
Pick the one that's better for you as the owner, tell me what you expect to end up with, and tell
me who covers the shortfall if the risky one pays 85."

## What to expect

This has not been run with students. Most students will probably compute the safe payoff (15)
correctly and miss the risky one, either answering 15 because "both pay 105" or forgetting that
owners stop at zero. In round 1 expect a majority to pick risky, some for the right reason and some
because risk sounds like return; the predictions tell you which. In the demo, 20 simulated
students picked risky 60% of the time when insured and 38% when priced.

**If round 1 is mostly safe,** ask the safe-pickers to compute the risky payoff; most will find
17.5 and then ask why they would not take it. **If round 2 is still mostly risky,** that is fine:
owners are indifferent under fair pricing, and the point is that the extra 2.5 is gone, not that
safe becomes the only right choice.

## Debrief questions

**On screen.** **Begin debrief** puts three things on the projector and on every phone: an *explain* prompt that quotes this round's numbers, a *transfer* question, and a private exit check (one question on the phone; the reasoning and a common mistake appear after the student answers). Give pairs about two minutes for the first two, then a minute for the exit check. Exit-check answers are not recorded, graded, or timed. When you show the next round, the projector also lists what changed from the last round and what stayed fixed, and asks for a prediction before anyone plays. The questions below go deeper.

- Both portfolios pay 105 on average. Where does the insured owners' extra 2.5 come from? (The
  insurer pays 5 half the time.)
- Depositors got 90 in every state of round 1. Why don't they care what the bank picks? Connect
  this to Bank Run: insurance that removes the reason to run also removes the reason to watch.
- In round 2, what did pricing take away? What would happen if lenders could not see the choice
  until after they had lent?
- Section 14's savings and loans: owners of an insolvent thrift had nothing left to lose. In this
  model, what happens to the owners' incentive as their equity falls toward zero? (Round 3 runs
  the other direction.)
- Transfer: an insurer charges 2.5 only to banks that pick risky. What do owners choose? What could
  a supervisor do instead of a premium? Does either make banking riskless?

Keep the claims conditional. This is a two-state model with risk-neutral owners; it does not show
that capital or insurance removes all banking risk, and pricing by lenders is a benchmark that
needs them to see the risk before they lend.

**Exit check:** with insured deposits of 90 and a flat premium, owners expect 17.5 from risky and
15 from safe. The extra 2.5 comes from the insurer, which pays 5 half the time. The common mistakes
are thinking the risky assets earn more on average and thinking insured depositors bear the loss.

## Fallback without the app

Put the model and the payoff table on a slide with the risky owner column blank. Ask for safe or
risky by show of hands, have students write their predicted ending equity and who covers the bad
state, then fill in the table.
